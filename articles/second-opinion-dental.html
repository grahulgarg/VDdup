When to Get a Second Opinion on a Dental Treatment Plan

No reasonable dentist minds being checked. If yours does, that is itself the answer.

By Dr. Pragati Singh, B.D.S., M.D.S. — Endodontist  ·  31 July 2026  ·  Vital Dental Care & Implant Center, Dehradun

Dentistry involves judgement, and competent dentists genuinely differ on borderline cases. A second opinion is not an accusation. It is ordinary prudence on anything expensive or irreversible, and it is standard practice in every other branch of medicine.

Situations clearly worth a second look

  • You have been told to extract a tooth, particularly a front tooth or one that is not loose. Extraction is irreversible, and an endodontist may see a way to save what a general plan writes off.
  • A large plan appeared at a first visit — a full-mouth proposal for someone who came in about one painful tooth.
  • Multiple crowns or veneers on healthy teeth for cosmetic reasons. This removes tooth structure permanently.
  • A quote far above or far below others, in either direction.
  • A root canal has failed and extraction is proposed. Re-treatment often works.
  • You do not understand why the treatment is needed, and asking did not clarify it.
  • Something feels rushed. Pressure to decide today is not a clinical argument.

When it is probably unnecessary

Routine fillings, cleanings, a straightforward extraction of a badly broken tooth, or an obvious abscess needing urgent treatment. Delaying genuine urgency to shop around is its own risk.

What to bring

Bring your X-rays — you are entitled to copies, and a second opinion based on the same images is far more useful, besides sparing you another dose of radiation. Bring the written treatment plan and quote, and a note of any medicines you take.

One suggestion: consider not saying what the first plan was until after the examination. You will get an independent view rather than a reaction to somebody else's.

Questions worth asking

  • What happens if I do nothing for six months?
  • Is there a less invasive option, even if it lasts less long?
  • Which parts are urgent and which can wait?
  • What would you do if this were your own tooth?

When two opinions differ

They often will, and both can be defensible. Ask each to explain the reasoning rather than the price. The plan that removes less tooth, keeps options open for later, and comes with a clear explanation of the alternatives is usually the sounder one. If the difference is stark, a third opinion is not excessive for major work.

Our position

We give second opinions routinely, including on plans from other clinics, and we will tell you plainly if we think the original plan was right — which happens often. As an endodontist-led practice we are biased in one specific direction, and it is worth stating: we would rather try to save a tooth than replace it. If your tooth genuinely cannot be saved, we will say that too.

Frequently asked questions

Is it rude to get a second dental opinion?

No, and no reasonable dentist objects. It is routine for expensive or irreversible treatment. A dentist who reacts badly to being checked has told you something useful.

Can I get copies of my dental X-rays?

Yes. They are part of your record and you are entitled to copies. Bringing them means the second opinion is based on the same evidence and you avoid unnecessary repeat radiation.

What if the two opinions completely disagree?

Ask each dentist to explain the reasoning rather than the price. Generally prefer the plan that removes less tooth structure and keeps future options open. For major work, a third opinion is reasonable.

Do you give second opinions on other clinics' treatment plans?

Yes, routinely, and we will tell you if we think the original plan was sound. Bring your X-rays and the written plan.
