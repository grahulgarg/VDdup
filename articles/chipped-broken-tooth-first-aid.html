Chipped or Broken a Tooth? What to Do Before You Reach a Dentist

Some breaks can wait a week. Others are urgent, and you can tell them apart by what you can see.

By Dr. Pragati Singh, B.D.S., M.D.S. — Endodontist  ·  6 August 2026  ·  Vital Dental Care & Implant Center, Dehradun

Teeth break in ways that range from cosmetically annoying to genuinely urgent. The useful distinction is how deep the fracture goes.

First aid, in order

  • Save the fragment in milk or saline. A clean break can sometimes be bonded back with the original piece, which looks better than any filling material.
  • Rinse with warm water to clear debris.
  • Cold compress on the cheek for swelling — twenty minutes on, twenty off.
  • Cover a sharp edge with orthodontic wax or a piece of sugar-free chewing gum so it does not lacerate your tongue or cheek.
  • Painkillers if needed, preferably an anti-inflammatory.
  • Do not chew on that side until it has been assessed.

How urgent is it?

Look at the broken surface.

Enamel only — a small chip, white throughout, no sensitivity. Not urgent. Book a normal appointment.

Into dentine — a yellowish layer visible, sensitive to cold and air. Should be seen within a few days; exposed dentine lets bacteria travel toward the nerve.

Pulp exposed — a pink or red dot in the middle of the broken surface, possibly with a spot of bleeding, usually painful. Urgent. Seen quickly, the nerve can sometimes be protected and saved; left a few days, it becomes a root canal.

Broken at or below the gumline, or a tooth that is loose or displaced after trauma — urgent, and it may need splinting.

Cracks you cannot see

A cracked tooth often shows nothing on examination and nothing on X-ray. The giveaway is a sharp pain when you release a bite rather than when you press down, often on one specific spot, sometimes with cold sensitivity. Cracks propagate — treated early a crown can hold the tooth together; left long enough the crack reaches the root and the tooth cannot be saved. If you have this symptom, do not wait for it to become obvious.

How breaks are repaired

  • Composite bonding — tooth-coloured material shaped and cured in one visit. Ideal for small to moderate chips on front teeth.
  • Reattaching the fragment — where you saved it and the break is clean.
  • Veneer — a thin ceramic facing for larger front-tooth damage.
  • Crown — where a substantial part of a back tooth has broken away, or to hold a cracked tooth together.
  • Root canal then crown — where the pulp is exposed or has died.

Why teeth break

Rarely out of nowhere. The common causes are a large old filling that has left thin walls of remaining tooth, an unprotected root-canal-treated tooth, night grinding, biting something hard (ice, nuts, unpopped popcorn kernels, bottle caps), or acid erosion thinning the enamel over years. If you have broken more than one tooth, the cause is worth investigating rather than just repairing the damage.

Frequently asked questions

Can a chipped tooth repair itself?

No. Enamel is not living tissue and does not regrow. A small chip may cause no problems, but the lost structure will not come back.

Will a bonded repair on a front tooth be visible?

Composite is shade-matched and polished, and a good repair is difficult to spot. It can stain slightly over several years with tea, coffee and turmeric, and can be polished or replaced.

I broke a tooth but it does not hurt. Do I still need to see a dentist?

Yes. A break with no pain usually means the nerve is not yet involved — which is exactly the point at which the simplest repair works. Waiting for pain means waiting for a bigger treatment.

What if the broken tooth is a back tooth with a large old filling?

That is the classic pattern. The remaining walls are thin and one of them has fractured. These usually need a crown, sometimes preceded by root canal treatment if the break is deep.
