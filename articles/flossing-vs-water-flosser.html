Floss or Water Flosser: Which Actually Cleans Better?

They are not the same tool and they do not do the same job. Which one you need depends on what is in your mouth.

By Dr. Pragati Singh, B.D.S., M.D.S. — Endodontist  ·  16 July 2026  ·  Vital Dental Care & Implant Center, Dehradun

A toothbrush cannot reach roughly two of the five surfaces of every tooth. Those contact points between teeth are where most adult decay begins and where gum disease takes hold. Something has to clean them — the only real question is what.

What string floss does well

Floss physically scrapes the tooth surface. That mechanical contact is what disrupts the biofilm — the sticky bacterial layer — that water alone tends to flow past. For tight contacts between healthy natural teeth, nothing removes plaque from the flat surfaces better.

Its weaknesses are practical: it is fiddly, most people do it badly or not at all, and it is difficult around braces, bridges and implants.

What a water flosser does well

A pulsating jet flushes debris and bacteria out of places floss struggles to enter. The evidence is consistently strong for reducing gum bleeding and inflammation, and it is markedly better than floss for:

  • Braces — around brackets and under wires.
  • Implants, bridges and crowns — where floss cannot pass under the join.
  • Deep gum pockets, which floss does not reach.
  • Anyone with dexterity problems, including arthritis.

Its weakness is that it is better at flushing than at scraping, so for tight healthy contacts it is a complement rather than a straight replacement.

The honest answer

The best interdental cleaner is the one you will actually use every day. A water flosser used nightly beats floss that stays in the drawer, by a wide margin. If you have natural teeth with tight contacts and you will genuinely floss, floss. If you have braces, implants or bridges, use a water flosser. If you can do both, do both — floss first, then flush.

The option most people have not tried

For anyone with slightly larger gaps — which includes most adults over 40 and anyone with some gum recession — interdental brushes outperform both. They are small bristle brushes in graded sizes that clean the sides of the teeth properly. Ask which size fits your gaps; using one that is too small does very little.

Doing it correctly

With floss, curve it into a C-shape against one tooth and slide gently under the gum, then repeat against the neighbouring tooth. Do not snap it down — that cuts the gum. With a water flosser, aim at the gumline at 90 degrees, lean over the sink with your mouth slightly open, and work along methodically. Either way, do it once a day, and preferably before brushing so the fluoride reaches the surfaces you have just cleaned.

Frequently asked questions

Can a water flosser replace flossing completely?

For most people with braces, bridges or implants, effectively yes. For tight contacts between healthy natural teeth, floss still removes plaque from flat surfaces more thoroughly, so using both is ideal.

Should I floss before or after brushing?

Before. Cleaning between the teeth first means the fluoride from your toothpaste can reach those surfaces afterwards, rather than being blocked by plaque.

Is it normal for gums to bleed when I start flossing?

For the first week or two, yes — it reflects existing inflammation. If it is still bleeding after two weeks of daily cleaning, it needs a professional assessment.

Are interdental brushes better than floss?

Where the gaps are large enough to take one, yes — the evidence favours them. They are only effective in the correct size, so ask to be shown which fits your mouth.
