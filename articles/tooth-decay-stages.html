The Five Stages of Tooth Decay — and the One That Still Reverses

Decay does not appear overnight. It moves through predictable stages, and only the first can be undone.

By Dr. Pragati Singh, B.D.S., M.D.S. — Endodontist  ·  18 June 2026  ·  Vital Dental Care & Implant Center, Dehradun

The single most useful fact about tooth decay is that it is reversible at the beginning and never reversible afterwards. Understanding where that line sits is what saves people fillings.

Stage 1: demineralisation

Bacteria in plaque turn sugar into acid, and that acid pulls calcium and phosphate out of the enamel. It shows as a chalky white spot, usually near the gumline. There is no hole and no pain. This stage reverses. Fluoride, better cleaning and less frequent sugar allow the enamel to take minerals back up from saliva. This is the entire reason for six-monthly check-ups — white spots are only visible to someone looking for them.

Stage 2: enamel decay

If demineralisation continues, the enamel surface collapses and a cavity forms. Enamel has no nerve supply, so there is still often no pain. From here it cannot heal — enamel is not living tissue and does not regrow. A small filling is all that is needed, and this is the cheapest and least invasive point at which to intervene.

Stage 3: dentine decay

Past the enamel is dentine, which is softer and decays considerably faster. This is usually when people first notice something: sensitivity to cold and sweet things, or a visible dark spot. A filling still works, but the cavity is bigger and closer to the nerve.

Stage 4: pulp involvement

Once bacteria reach the pulp, the nerve becomes inflamed. Now there is real pain — often spontaneous, often worse at night, often lingering after cold. A filling will not help; the inflamed tissue has to be removed. This is root canal territory.

Stage 5: abscess

The pulp dies and infection travels out of the root tip into the bone, forming an abscess. There may be swelling, a bad taste, a pimple on the gum, fever, or a tooth that feels raised when you bite. Treatment is root canal or extraction, and it is the point at which a dental problem can become a medical one.

What actually prevents it

Frequency of sugar matters more than quantity. One dessert eaten in ten minutes causes one acid attack; the same sugar sipped in tea across the afternoon causes many, and the mouth never gets back to neutral. Fluoride toothpaste twice daily, cleaning between the teeth, and not rinsing your mouth with water immediately after brushing — spit, do not rinse, so the fluoride stays on the teeth — do most of the work.

The rest is being seen twice a year, because stage one is silent and stage four is not.

Frequently asked questions

Can a cavity heal on its own?

Only at the white-spot stage, before the enamel surface has broken. Once there is an actual hole, the tooth cannot repair it and a filling is needed.

Why does my tooth have a hole but no pain?

Enamel has no nerves, so decay is usually painless until it reaches dentine or the pulp. Absence of pain is not evidence of absence of decay — it usually means it is early, which is good news.

How often should I have dental X-rays for decay?

Typically every one to two years for adults at average risk, more often if you have had recent decay. Bitewing X-rays find decay between teeth, which is invisible to the eye.

Does sugar-free mean tooth-friendly?

Not always. Many sugar-free drinks are highly acidic, and acid erodes enamel directly without needing bacteria. Check for citric or phosphoric acid, not just sugar.
